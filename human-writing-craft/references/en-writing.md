# English writing and de-slopping guide

English “AI voice” is rarely one forbidden word. It usually appears as a cluster: inflated significance, vague attribution, symmetrical argument shapes, abstract nouns, polished neutrality, and a conclusion that rises above the evidence.

Use this guide to diagnose function and density. Do not run a blind synonym swap, and do not infer authorship from style.

## 1. High-value review leads

### Inflated significance

Common shapes include:

- `marks a pivotal moment`
- `serves as a testament to`
- `underscores the importance of`
- `represents a broader shift`
- `shapes the evolving landscape`
- `stands as a beacon`

The problem is not elevated language by itself. It is importance without a named consequence.

Ask:

- What changed?
- For whom?
- Compared with what?
- What evidence supports the significance claim?

Before:

> The launch marks a pivotal moment in the evolving landscape of developer productivity.

After, when supported:

> The launch puts code review and deployment logs in the same workspace, so developers no longer switch between three tools during routine releases.

Do not invent the workflow details. If the source only says the product launched, use a plain sentence.

### Vague attribution

Watch for:

- `studies show`
- `experts say`
- `industry reports suggest`
- `critics argue`
- `users have reported`
- `it is widely believed`

Use a named source when the material provides one. Otherwise:

1. remove the attribution if the claim stands independently;
2. mark the claim as unsupported;
3. or remove the entire claim.

Never create a source to make the sentence look researched.

### Promotional language outside a promotional job

Clusters such as `vibrant`, `breathtaking`, `groundbreaking`, `world-class`, `seamless`, `cutting-edge`, `must-visit`, and `game-changing` often advertise instead of describe.

Marketing copy may legitimately persuade. It still needs a specific benefit, proof, audience, and limitation. A product is not “seamless” merely because setup has three steps instead of four.

### Formulaic challenge and future sections

A generic article often adds a paragraph shaped like:

> Despite these challenges, the future remains bright as the organization continues to innovate.

Keep a challenge only when the piece names it. Keep an outlook only when there is a dated plan, forecast, decision, or unresolved question. Delete ritual optimism.

### Staged contrast

Repeated forms such as `not X, but Y`, `it isn't just X; it's Y`, and `less X, more Y` can create manufactured depth.

Do not ban negation. Rewrite when the first half invents a position nobody held or when the same reveal cadence keeps returning.

Before:

> This isn't just a faster search tool. It's a new way to think about knowledge.

After:

> The search tool returns cited passages and keeps earlier queries in the same workspace.

Again, only use supported features.

### Shallow participial add-ons

Watch for clauses beginning with `highlighting`, `underscoring`, `showcasing`, `reflecting`, `ensuring`, or `fostering` that claim analysis without adding evidence.

> The team opened a second office, showcasing its commitment to global growth.

Possible revision:

> The team opened a second office in Toronto to support North American customers.

If the source does not state the purpose, stop after `The team opened a second office in Toronto.`

### Chatbot residue

Remove wrappers that belong to the assistant interaction rather than the artifact:

- `Of course!`
- `Great question.`
- `Here is a comprehensive overview.`
- `I hope this helps.`
- `Let me know if you would like...`
- knowledge-cutoff explanations or tool-process narration that the publication does not need

A customer email or conversational reply may need a greeting or closing. Match the actual genre rather than deleting politeness globally.

## 2. Contextual pattern families

### Cliché vocabulary

Words such as `delve`, `leverage`, `robust`, `comprehensive`, `landscape`, `tapestry`, `pivotal`, `intricate`, `seamless`, `navigate`, and `unpack` are review leads only when they cluster or substitute for specifics.

Legitimate uses remain legitimate:

- `robust regression`
- `financial leverage`
- a physical `landscape`
- `unpack` as a literal software operation
- `comprehensive metabolic panel`

Prefer a direct verb when the inflated word adds no precision. Do not replace terminology because a list told you to.

### Em dashes

An em dash can be a writer's normal punctuation. A pileup can create a familiar model cadence, especially when every dash delivers a dramatic aside or correction.

Use the writer's samples and publication style. Reduce repeated function, not the glyph itself. Do not impose a zero-dash rule unless the house style requires it.

### Forced groups of three

Triads are memorable and common in human rhetoric. Flag them when every section manufactures three items, especially three abstract nouns with no evidence.

Preserve real three-part taxonomies, legal tests, research variables, and product tiers.

### Passive voice

Passive voice becomes a problem when it hides responsibility:

> Mistakes were made during the migration.

It is useful when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, already established, or standard in the genre:

> Samples were stored at −80 °C.

Find the actor only when the source provides one.

### Nominalization

`conducted an evaluation of` can often become `evaluated`. But nominalization is normal in legal, scientific, and technical prose. Change it when it obscures action or adds empty formality, not merely because it exists.

### Sentence and paragraph variation

Flat rhythm can make prose feel generated. Random variation can make it worse. Change cadence when several adjacent units perform the same function in the same shape.

Do not use sentence-length variance as an authorship test or force arbitrary one-sentence paragraphs.

### Rhetorical questions

A repeated question-answer pattern can sound instructional or synthetic. A well-placed question can also be the cleanest way to frame uncertainty. Judge frequency, function, and voice.

## 3. Structural problems

### The introduction announces instead of beginning

Weak:

> In this article, we will explore the many ways artificial intelligence is transforming modern software development.

Stronger:

> Code review changed first. Teams could generate a plausible patch in minutes, then spend longer proving that it was safe.

Use the stronger opening only when the material supports the observation.

### The outline writes the article

A default `three benefits / three risks / future outlook` shape often expands a thin idea rather than develops it. Let sections follow actual turns in the evidence or argument.

Each section should add at least one of:

- a fact
- an example
- a distinction
- a causal step
- a counterexample
- a decision or consequence

A heading that merely renames the topic is not progress.

### Synonym cycling

AI drafts often rename the same object to avoid repetition:

> the platform, the solution, the technology, the innovative system

Use the stable term. Repetition is safer than implying four different entities.

### Every paragraph lands like a quote card

A sequence of short, polished conclusions creates a recognizable humanizer voice. Let ordinary paragraphs end with evidence, a qualification, or a transition. Reserve the sharp one-liner for a moment that earns it.

## 4. Density, not prohibition

A useful audit distinguishes three levels:

- **Single hit:** usually keep unless it is clearly empty or wrong.
- **Cluster:** two or more related patterns in one paragraph deserve review.
- **Document habit:** the same move recurs across sections and shapes the voice.

A list of “AI words” should never become an authorship score. It is a map for close reading.

## 5. Positive English targets

After removing templates, aim for:

- a clear actor and action where the source supports them
- specific nouns, examples, conditions, and consequences
- stable terminology
- direct claims with nearby evidence
- uncertainty stated at the right strength
- transitions earned by logic
- sentence variation driven by thought
- a conclusion that finishes the argument rather than elevates it

Human prose is not necessarily casual, quirky, or imperfect. A precise technical explanation can sound fully authored without a single joke or personal aside.

## 6. Read-aloud pass

Read the draft aloud or simulate that pass carefully. Listen for:

- identical clause length and stress
- repeated `Here's the thing`, `The truth is`, or `What matters is` openings
- a sequence of reveal pivots
- abstract nouns where a person or system should act
- breathless long sentences with several decorative clauses
- staccato fragments added solely to look human
- the same “punch line” ending in every paragraph

If it sounds like a keynote, a LinkedIn carousel, or an edited-by-humanizer house style, revise the repeated function. If it sounds ordinary and accurate, ordinary is enough.
